Case Context

Copper Cup Coffee is considering a three-year traceability system for its featured coffee. The system would connect lot records, supplier documents, and customer-facing information. The vendor requires the full $9,500 before implementation, but the system's possible cash benefits would arrive gradually over the next three years. Owner Elena Torres has not approved the project or chosen how to fund it. This finance case begins with that open decision.

The system is an intangible investment: customers cannot hold the software or records, but the system may improve verification, reduce information gaps, and support a differentiated product. Those possible benefits still have to justify the cash committed today.

Investment Estimate

The full system costs $9,500 today. Maya estimates net incremental cash benefits of $3,800 in Year 1, $4,200 in Year 2, and $4,600 in Year 3. The project team proposes an 8% discount rate for the base analysis.

Funding Estimate

Copper Cup has $18,000 in available cash reserves, which also supports ordinary needs such as payroll, inventory, rent, and unexpected repairs. Paying the full cost in cash would use more than half of that reserve and leave $8,500. Copper Cup is therefore considering a $9,500, three-year loan at 9% with equal annual payments. Borrowing would preserve cash at the start, but it would add interest and required payments. A smaller pilot is also available for $5,000 today.

All figures are fictional, simplified, and stated before taxes. They are estimates for learning, not promises. The base case excludes financing cash flows so that the project itself can be valued separately from the choice of how to pay for it.

Finance decision: Does the traceability project create value at the proposed discount rate, what risks could change that conclusion, and which funding approach best fits Copper Cup's liquidity needs?

Finance: When the Bean Becomes an Investment

A coffee bean passes through farms, processors, exporters, importers, roasters, and cafes. At every stage, someone pays money now and hopes to receive benefits later. Finance helps businesses compare those costs, benefits, timing, and risks.

Copper Cup Needs Money Now

Copper Cup wants a traceability system that would connect coffee-lot records, supplier documents, and customer information. The vendor requires $9,500 before installation. The expected savings and added sales would arrive gradually over the next three years.

Copper Cup has $18,000 in cash reserves, but that money also supports payroll, coffee inventory, rent, and unexpected repairs. Paying the full cost today would leave $8,500. Elena is therefore considering a loan. Borrowing would not make the system more valuable, but it could preserve cash for daily operations.

Two Different Decisions

Maya first separates two questions. The investment decision asks, “Is the traceability system worth purchasing?” The financing decision asks, “If Copper Cup purchases it, should the cafe use cash, borrow money, or begin with a smaller pilot?”

Keeping these questions separate prevents a common mistake. Maya first studies the project's cash flows, meaning the cash Copper Cup expects to pay or receive because of the project. Then she compares ways to pay for it.

What this means: A worthwhile project can still create financial pressure if its loan payments or upfront cost leave the business short of cash.

What Is Future Money Worth Today?

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar received later because today's dollar can earn a return and future cash flows are uncertain.

Present value (PV) expresses future money in today's dollars. Copper Cup expects a $4,600 benefit in Year 3. At an 8% discount rate, that future benefit is worth about $3,652 today.

Net present value (NPV) compares the project's cost today with the present value of all expected future benefits. Copper Cup's three benefits have a combined present value of about $10,771. After subtracting the $9,500 cost, the base-case NPV is about +$1,271.

What this means: At the 8% rate, the estimated benefits are $1,271 greater than the cost in today's dollars. On the Value Over Time tab, change the rate and watch the result change.

A Positive NPV Is Not a Guarantee

The calculation depends on estimates. Copper Cup does not know exactly how much the system will save, how customers will respond, or whether implementation will go as planned.

That distinction matters because risk is uncertainty about future outcomes.

If the benefits are only $2,500, $2,800, and $3,000, the project's NPV at 8% becomes about -$2,403. The negative result warns that the estimated benefits would no longer cover the project's cost at the required return.

What this means: Positive NPV supports the investment only under the assumptions used. Maya must test more than one possible outcome.

Cash or Loan?

Paying $9,500 in cash avoids interest, but it reduces Copper Cup's reserve to $8,500. Borrowing preserves cash at the start, but the proposed loan requires payments of about $3,753 each year for three years and adds about $1,759 in interest.

Liquidity means having cash available to meet near-term obligations. NPV helps Maya evaluate the project. It does not tell her whether Copper Cup can comfortably make the loan payments while also paying employees, suppliers, rent, and other expenses.

The goal of financial management is commonly framed as increasing the value of the firm.

For Copper Cup, increasing value means more than selecting the largest forecast. Elena must consider the expected return, the downside risk, the cash the cafe needs to keep operating, and the people affected by the decision. Value Over Time

Finance translates cash flows received at different times into today's dollars so they can be compared with the $9,500 Copper Cup must pay now. Use the explorer to see how time and the discount rate change the estimated value.

Finance Terms Used Here
Cash flow
Cash received or paid by the business. Example: The $9,500 system cost is a cash outflow; the estimated $3,800 Year 1 benefit is a cash inflow.
Future value
The amount that money available today could grow to through compounding. Example: $100 invested at 8% would have a future value of $108 after one year.
Present value (PV)
The value today of cash expected in the future. Example: Copper Cup's $4,600 Year 3 benefit is worth about $3,652 today at an 8% discount rate.
Discounting
Moving a future amount back to its equivalent value today. Example: Discounting turns the $3,800 Year 1 benefit into about $3,519 in today's dollars at 8%.
Discount rate
The required return used to account for time and the risk being evaluated. Example: Raising the rate from 8% to 16% lowers the present value of Copper Cup's future benefits.
Net present value (NPV)
The present value of future project cash flows minus the initial investment. Example: At 8%, $10,771 in present-value benefits minus the $9,500 cost produces an NPV of about +$1,271.

Five Checks Before Trusting the NPV

  1. Use incremental cash flows.
    Include only changes caused by the project.
  2. Place cash flows correctly.
    State when each amount occurs.
  3. Match rate and timing.
    Use consistent annual or periodic units.
  4. Test uncertainty.
    Change material assumptions and recalculate.
  5. Audit the model.
    Trace formulas, inputs, sources, and rounding.

Why the Loan Is a Separate Decision

A $9,500, three-year loan at 9% with equal annual payments requires about $3,753 per year. Total payments are about $11,259, including about $1,759 of interest. Paying cash avoids that interest but leaves $8,500 in reserves; paying cash for the pilot leaves $13,000. The PV and NPV explorer evaluates the project before financing. Copper Cup must separately decide whether preserving cash is worth the loan's interest and fixed payment obligation.

Risk, Return, and Governance

Risk is uncertainty about what will occur. Finance makes uncertainty visible through scenarios, sensitivity analysis, required returns, portfolio thinking, and governance controls.

1

Operating Risk

Benefits may fall if adoption, savings, data quality, or execution disappoints.

2

Financial Risk

Debt creates fixed payments even when project cash flows are weak.

3

Liquidity Risk

Using cash may leave too little flexibility for payroll, repairs, or shocks.

4

Information Risk

Incomplete, biased, or unverified inputs can make a precise model misleading.

Diversification Has Limits

An investor can combine assets whose outcomes do not move together, reducing company-specific risk. Broad economic or market shocks remain. Copper Cup cannot call this single project diversified; it can limit concentration by preserving liquidity, staging exposure, and avoiding dependence on one estimate or vendor.

Agency and Behavioral Safeguards

An agency conflict can arise when the person recommending a project benefits from approval but does not bear the full cost of failure. Governance uses incentives, oversight, approval rights, disclosure, and monitoring to align the decision. Copper Cup can require a second reviewer to trace formulas and sources, document who approved assumptions, and set post-investment checkpoints.

Behavioral safeguards address predictable judgment errors. A prewritten stop rule helps counter the disposition effect, or the tendency to hold a losing investment too long. Base and downside cases counter overconfidence. A dissenting review challenges confirmation bias. Scheduled evidence reviews help when limited attention causes decision makers to overlook changes.

ESG and Climate Evidence

Environmental, social, and governance factors belong in an investment model when evidence connects them to cash flows or risk. A climate disruption may affect supply reliability, costs, and asset values. A governance weakness may reduce confidence in records. The analyst should estimate the mechanism and uncertainty rather than assign automatic value to an ESG label.

AI at Work: Challenge the Forecast

AI can generate adverse scenarios, compare risk disclosures, or surface assumptions that deserve testing. Maya must check source quality, recency, omitted variables, and invented citations. Sensitive company or supplier data should be handled only in approved systems.

Investment and Financing Decision

Elena has three defensible paths to compare. None is automatically correct without a minimum-liquidity requirement, confidence in the estimates, and a clear view of downside tolerance.

Option A: Full System, Paid With Cash

Financial evidence: Base-case project NPV is about +$1,271; no loan interest is incurred; cash reserves fall from $18,000 to $8,500.

Decision tension: Copper Cup captures the full estimated benefit but gives up more financial flexibility today.

Option B: Full System, Funded With Debt

Financial evidence: The same operating project is evaluated separately; financing requires about $3,753 annually for three years and about $1,759 total interest.

Decision tension: Cash is preserved initially, but fixed payments increase financial risk when benefits are uncertain.

Option C: Smaller Pilot, Paid With Cash

Financial evidence: Pilot NPV is about +$808; initial cost is $5,000; cash reserves remain at $13,000.

Decision tension: Copper Cup preserves liquidity and buys information, but may delay or forgo some full-system benefits.

Case Prompt

Recommend one funding path only after stating the minimum cash reserve, the most decision-sensitive assumption, and a governance safeguard. Explain how your recommendation changes if the downside scenario becomes more likely.
